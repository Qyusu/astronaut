## General Background

- **Background**: The Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) is a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm introduced in 2014 as a way to find ground-state energies of molecular Hamiltonians using near-term quantum devices ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=To%20address%20this%20problem%2C%20a,23%20Harsha%20et%20al)). Instead of deep circuits required by algorithms like Quantum Phase Estimation, VQE uses a *trial wavefunction* or **ansatz** – a parametrized quantum circuit – which is optimized variationally to minimize the measured energy of the system ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=factors%3A%20the%20complexity%20of%20the,categories%E2%80%94chemistry%E2%80%93inspired%20and%20hardware%E2%80%93efficient%E2%80%94that%20produce%20quantum)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=To%20address%20this%20problem%2C%20a,23%20Harsha%20et%20al)). The ansatz prepares a guess for the molecular ground state on the quantum processor, and a classical optimizer tunes its parameters to minimize the energy. This approach leverages the variational principle and shifts the burden to finding a good ansatz that can represent the true ground state with limited quantum resources ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=successfully%20applied%20to%20solve%20the,recent%20developments%20in%20the%20field)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Shorter%20circuits%20make%20VQE%20more,kind%20lies%20between%20the%20chemistry)). Historically, the idea of using a parametrized trial state comes from the **variational method** in quantum mechanics – a technique long used in quantum chemistry where one assumes a form of the wavefunction and optimizes its parameters to approximate the ground state energy. For instance, early quantum chemists used Slater determinants (antisymmetrized products of orbitals) as ansätze for atoms and molecules, optimizing orbital shapes to lower the energy (the Hartree–Fock method) ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=Two%20highly%20productive%20approaches%20to,the%20localized%20version%20of%20this)) ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=Coupled%20cluster%20methods%20are%20the,Thus)). The concept of an ansatz thus predates quantum computing and is fundamental: it’s effectively a guess for the wavefunction structure, informed by physical insight or mathematical convenience.

- **Current State**: In the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.04913#:~:text=Although%20Quantum%20computing%20has%20made,7%5D%2C%20and%20quantum)), ansatz design is *critical* because current quantum hardware is limited in qubit count and coherence, meaning only relatively shallow circuits can run reliably ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.04913#:~:text=However%2C%20VQA%20still%20faces%20many,and%20accuracy%20of%20the%20algorithm)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=of%20the%20variational%20ansatz%20used,how%20they%20are%20addressed%20in)). The accuracy and scalability of VQE are largely determined by the quality of the ansatz ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=successfully%20applied%20to%20solve%20the,recent%20developments%20in%20the%20field)). A well-chosen ansatz can capture essential electron correlation with fewer parameters and gates, making it easier to reach the true ground state before noise and errors dominate ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1420-3049/30/1/119#:~:text=The%20choice%20of%20Ansatz%20in,the%20Ansatz%20to%20the%20specific)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1420-3049/30/1/119#:~:text=characteristics%20of%20the%20quantum%20system,variational%20algorithms%20in%20quantum%20computing)). Research has converged on two broad categories of ansätze for molecular problems ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Shorter%20circuits%20make%20VQE%20more,kind%20lies%20between%20the%20chemistry)). **Chemistry-inspired ansätze** incorporate domain knowledge from traditional quantum chemistry – for example, the Unitary Coupled Cluster (UCC) family draws from the coupled cluster wavefunction used in classical chemistry ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20first%20VQE%20experiment%20by,hat%20%7BT%7D%5E%7B%5Cdagger)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=%24%24%20%5Chat,dagg%20er%7D_%7Bj%7D%5Chat%7Ba%7D_%7B%5Cbeta%7D%20%5Chat%7Ba%7D_%7B%5Calpha%7D.)). These ansätze explicitly map to electron excitations and often start from a Hartree–Fock reference, thus naturally respecting physical symmetries like fermionic antisymmetry and particle number. In contrast, **hardware-efficient ansätze** are problem-agnostic circuits composed of layers of parameterized gates that are easily implementable on a given quantum hardware topology ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Hardware,the%20%E2%80%9Cbarren%20plateaus%E2%80%9D%20of%20the)). They sacrifice a clear chemical interpretation in favor of short-depth circuits that leverage native gate operations. A third, intermediate approach sometimes cited is the **Hamiltonian Variational Ansatz**, which builds the circuit using operators taken from the system Hamiltonian (similar in spirit to Trotterized time evolution or QAOA) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Shorter%20circuits%20make%20VQE%20more,kind%20lies%20between%20the%20chemistry)). This approach uses problem Hamiltonian terms to inform the circuit structure, aiming to balance expressiveness with hardware efficiency. Currently, the field is actively exploring *automated ansatz design* techniques, including adaptive algorithms and AI-driven methods, to tailor ansätze to each problem. For example, adaptive methods like ADAPT-VQE build the circuit incrementally based on the system’s chemistry ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ansatz%20Variational%20Quantum%20Eigensolver%2C%20or,the%20iterative%20algorithm%20for%20the)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L695%20The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)), and machine learning approaches (e.g. reinforcement learning agents) have been proposed to discover efficient circuit structures for VQE.

- **Challenges**: Despite progress, significant challenges remain in ansatz design for VQE. One major issue is the **trade-off between ansatz expressiveness and trainability** ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=hardware,efficient)). A more expressive ansatz (with many parameters and a wide range of states it can represent) is more likely to include the true ground state, but it also induces a larger search space that can be **barren** – i.e. flat regions in the parameter landscape where gradients vanish (the **barren plateau** problem) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=the%20current%20quantum%20hardware,This%20suggests%20that)). This is especially problematic in hardware-efficient ansätze with random initial parameters, where the objective function’s gradients tend to zero exponentially with circuit depth ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=the%20current%20quantum%20hardware,This%20suggests%20that)). Another challenge is **circuit depth and hardware errors**: Chemistry-inspired ansätze like UCCSD can require a large number of *entangling gates* (CNOTs) and deep circuits for even moderate molecules ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=%2A%20%22Hardware,Nature%20549%2C%20242%E2%80%93246%20%282017)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,but%20requires%20double%20the%20number)). Current hardware cannot execute such deep circuits with high fidelity, so there is a tension between physically accurate ansätze and hardware limitations ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=of%20the%20variational%20ansatz%20used,how%20they%20are%20addressed%20in)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=In%20Eq.%206%20%2C%20t_,constitute%20the%20ansatz%20more%20efficiently)). Limited qubit counts also constrain using larger basis sets or including many excited configurations in the ansatz. **Optimization difficulties** present another challenge: VQE’s classical optimization can get stuck in local minima or be sensitive to initial parameters. If the ansatz has many parameters (hundreds or more), optimizers may struggle, especially under noise. Additionally, ensuring the ansatz respects **symmetries and constraints** of the problem is difficult but important – e.g., an arbitrary hardware-efficient ansatz might explore state configurations with the wrong number of electrons or total spin if not carefully constrained ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=of%20the%20system%20being%20simulated%2C,This%20suggests%20that)). Enforcing such symmetries either requires adding penalty terms to the cost or designing the circuit with symmetry-preserving structure, both of which complicate ansatz design. Finally, there is the challenge of **scalability**: methods that work for small molecules (few qubits) may not scale smoothly. The number of measurements needed to evaluate energy grows with system size, and some advanced ansatz schemes (like adaptive ones) incur extra overhead by requiring many intermediate calculations (e.g. computing gradients for each new gate added in ADAPT-VQE) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L719%20count%2C%20which,each%20ADAPT%20iteration%20is%20equal)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=number%20of%20CNOT%20gates%20in,P%7D%3Di%5Cprod)). Summarizing, the key hurdles are balancing depth versus accuracy, avoiding untrainable parameter landscapes, and reducing resource overhead – all while navigating the constraints of NISQ hardware.

- **Insights & Recommendations**: Based on current understanding, a few guiding insights have emerged for ansatz design. First, **incorporating problem knowledge** is invaluable for efficiency – ansätze that start from physically meaningful states or excitation operators tend to reach chemical accuracy with far fewer parameters than completely generic circuits ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=parameters%20is%20too%20large.%20ADAPT,time%2C%20so%20every%20iteration%20of)). For instance, using the Hartree–Fock state as a starting point and including operators that correspond to electron excitations (as in UCC or ADAPT-VQE) focuses the ansatz on the relevant subspace of the full Hilbert space. This reduces the prevalence of barren plateaus since the initial parameters can be chosen near zero (perturbatively) rather than random, giving non-zero gradients ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=parameters%20is%20too%20large.%20ADAPT,time%2C%20so%20every%20iteration%20of)). On the other hand, **hardware-aware simplifications** are critical – each two-qubit gate is precious on NISQ devices. Thus, truncating insignificant terms or using compressed operator pools can dramatically cut down circuit depth with minimal loss in accuracy ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,but%20requires%20double%20the%20number)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)). Examples include the k-UpCCGSD ansatz which omits many weak excitation terms but still achieves high accuracy by concentrating on strong pair excitations ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,but%20requires%20double%20the%20number)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=state%20energy%20with%20a%20smaller,UpCCGSD)). Secondly, **adaptive ansätze** like ADAPT-VQE show that building the circuit iteratively can yield compact, problem-tailored circuits that outperform fixed ansatz in both accuracy and gate count ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=internuclear%20distances%2C%20the%20error%20gets,required%20parameters%20grows%20significantly%20for)). One recommendation is to use adaptive methods or circuit “learning” algorithms for larger, unfamiliar systems, since a fixed ansatz may either waste resources or miss essential correlations. However, one should balance the *overhead* of adaptive methods (many intermediate measurements and re-optimizations) with the gains in circuit efficiency – sometimes a well-chosen fixed ansatz with slight tweaking can be more practical. Third, **maintaining symmetry constraints** (particle number, spin, etc.) in the ansatz is recommended to avoid unphysical states and ease the optimization landscape ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=of%20the%20system%20being%20simulated%2C,This%20suggests%20that)). This can be done by construction (e.g. only include excitation operators that conserve particle number) or by adding explicit constraints/penalties in the cost function. Another insight is the importance of **parameter initialization**: random initial angles in a deep ansatz often land one in a barren plateau ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=the%20current%20quantum%20hardware,This%20suggests%20that)). Instead, setting initial parameters to zero or small values (so the ansatz begins near the reference state) or using classically pre-optimized values (when available) can improve the optimizer’s success. In summary, the recommended strategy for designing ansätze is to **strike a balance** – use as much domain knowledge as possible to keep the ansatz compact and relevant, enforce necessary physical constraints, but also keep it hardware-friendly by minimizing circuit depth. Where possible, leverage **algorithmic tools or AI** to automate this design, exploring hybrid approaches (e.g. a hardware-efficient backbone with a few problem-specific layers) to get the best of both worlds. As the field progresses, we anticipate more sophisticated ansatz-generation techniques (e.g. reinforcement learning agents for quantum circuit design) will help navigate the exponentially large design space and discover ansätze that are both efficient and effective for finding molecular ground states.

## Ansatz Comparison

Below we compare a range of ansatz types used for molecular ground-state VQE, highlighting their design principles, pros/cons, and how well they lend themselves to iterative optimization techniques:

- **Hardware-Efficient Ansatz (HEA)** – **Core structure**: Layered circuits composed of easy-to-implement gate blocks repeated in depth ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=The%20parametrized%20quantum%20circuit%20of,are%20applied%20only%20to%20adjacent)). Typically, each layer includes single-qubit rotations on all qubits (e.g. parameterized $R_y$ or $R_z$ gates) followed by a pattern of entangling two-qubit gates (e.g. CNOTs or CZs arranged in a nearest-neighbor chain or ring) ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=The%20parametrized%20quantum%20circuit%20of,are%20applied%20only%20to%20adjacent)). These gates are chosen to align with the hardware’s native operations and connectivity (hence “hardware-efficient”). **Advantages**: The HEA is *easy to deploy on NISQ hardware* – it requires relatively shallow circuits per layer and uses gates that are less prone to error on a given platform ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=easy%20to%20implement%20in%20real,found%20in%20the%20reference%20below)). It’s also *highly expressive*; with enough layers, a hardware-efficient ansatz can in principle approximate any state (the layers act like a universal approximator) and thus can represent diverse quantum states including the ground state of many systems. Moreover, it’s a *general-purpose form* – the same ansatz architecture can be used for different molecules or even different problem types without modification, simplifying its use. **Limitations**: The generic nature is also its weakness – HEAs lack built-in chemical knowledge, so they often require more parameters or layers to reach high accuracy for molecular problems ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=In%20Eq.%206%20%2C%20t_,constitute%20the%20ansatz%20more%20efficiently)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=hardware,efficient)). This can lead to *barren plateaus* where the optimization gets stuck because the random initial circuit is too entangled ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=the%20current%20quantum%20hardware,This%20suggests%20that)). Additionally, hardware-efficient ansätze do not inherently conserve particle number or other symmetries, meaning the optimization might wander into unphysical regions of state space ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=of%20the%20system%20being%20simulated%2C,This%20suggests%20that)). Enforcing such symmetries might require extra circuit elements or post-selection, complicating the approach. **Suitability for iterative optimization**: In principle, one can iteratively deepen a hardware-efficient ansatz by adding more layers until the energy converges (sometimes called layer-by-layer VQE). This is straightforward to implement, but the challenge is that adding layers increases parameter count, and if the landscape is already flat (barren), adding more might not help. Some iterative strategies initialize new layers to identity (parameters zero) to avoid disrupting the already optimized part – this can mitigate trainability issues. In summary, HEAs are well-suited to current hardware and can be grown iteratively, but require careful optimization techniques (and possibly layerwise training or smart initialization) to avoid the known training difficulties ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=the%20current%20quantum%20hardware,This%20suggests%20that)).

- **Unitary Coupled Cluster Ansatz (UCC)** – **Core structure**: A chemistry-inspired ansatz based on the traditional coupled cluster wavefunction from quantum chemistry ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20first%20VQE%20experiment%20by,hat%20%7BT%7D%5E%7B%5Cdagger)). In the UCC ansatz, one starts from a reference state (usually the Hartree–Fock determinant) and applies an operator $U(\vec{\theta}) = e^{T(\vec{\theta}) - T^\dagger(\vec{\theta})}$, where $T$ is a *cluster operator* that includes excitation terms which promote electrons from occupied orbitals to virtual (unoccupied) orbitals ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=UCC%20ansatz%20is%20a%20cousin,state%20of%20a%20given%20molecular)). For example, the popular UCCSD variant truncates $T = T_1 + T_2$ to single and double excitations with variational amplitudes for each allowed excitation ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=%24%24%20%5Chat,dagg%20er%7D_%7Bj%7D%5Chat%7Ba%7D_%7B%5Cbeta%7D%20%5Chat%7Ba%7D_%7B%5Calpha%7D.)). This exponential is then compiled into a sequence of quantum gates (usually via a Trotter decomposition) acting on qubits that represent those orbitals ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=%5Cfrac%7B1%7D%7B2%7D%5Csum_%7Bijkl%7D%20%5Ctheta_%7Bijkl%7D%20a_i,electron%20correlations%20in%20the%20molecule)). **Advantages**: UCC ansätze leverage known **physics of electron correlation**. Even truncated UCCSD often gives a very good approximation to molecular ground states by capturing essential two-electron interactions ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=%5Cfrac%7B1%7D%7B2%7D%5Csum_%7Bijkl%7D%20%5Ctheta_%7Bijkl%7D%20a_i,electron%20correlations%20in%20the%20molecule)). Because each term in $T$ corresponds to a specific electron excitation, every parameter has a clear physical meaning (unlike in hardware-efficient circuits). This structured approach means many unimportant configurations are automatically excluded, which can make optimization easier – the initial amplitudes can be set near zero (recovering mean-field as a starting point) so that the ansatz begins close to the Hartree–Fock energy and then systematically improves it ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=In%20Eq.%206%20%2C%20t_,constitute%20the%20ansatz%20more%20efficiently)). UCC ansätze also inherently preserve fermionic symmetries (like particle number and spin, if all relevant excitations are included but symmetry-violating terms are not) since they are built from fermionic creation/annihilation operators that commute with those symmetries ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=number%20of%20CNOT%20gates%20in,P%7D%3Di%5Cprod)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=compared%20with%20the%20fermionic%20ADAPT%2C,the%20strings%20with%20an%20even)). **Limitations**: The biggest drawback is *circuit depth*. A straightforward implementation of UCCSD for even moderate molecules can produce a very deep circuit with many CNOT gates ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=%2A%20%22Hardware,Nature%20549%2C%20242%E2%80%93246%20%282017)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=In%20Eq.%206%20%2C%20t_,constitute%20the%20ansatz%20more%20efficiently)). The number of terms (and parameters) in UCCSD grows combinatorially with system size – for instance, doubling the number of orbitals drastically increases the count of possible double excitations. Many of those excitation terms have extremely small amplitudes in correlation expansions (as classical CC experience shows) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=In%20Eq.%206%20%2C%20t_,constitute%20the%20ansatz%20more%20efficiently)), meaning there’s a lot of “wasted” effort implementing negligible rotations. This leads to unfavorable scaling: the gate count for UCCSD circuits can grow steeply (often quoted as exponential in system size for full UCC, though various truncation and reduction schemes exist) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=In%20Eq.%206%20%2C%20t_,constitute%20the%20ansatz%20more%20efficiently)). Moreover, the needed Trotter approximations introduce error unless many steps are used, further increasing depth (though in practice often a single Trotter step is used as an ansatz and treated variationally). **Suitability for iterative optimization**: Traditional UCCSD is a fixed ansatz, but one can imagine iteratively extending it by including higher excitations (UCCSDT for triples, etc.) to improve accuracy. Indeed, classical quantum chemistry uses such a hierarchy (CCSD, CCSD(T), CCSDTQ, etc.). On quantum hardware, including triples or quadruples directly is usually impractical due to even larger circuits. A more viable iterative approach is **adaptive or selective inclusion** of cluster operators – this is precisely what ADAPT-VQE does (see below). Short of full adaptivity, one can **prune** the UCC operator pool by some importance measure (for example, include only excitations above a certain estimated amplitude from perturbation theory) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=all%20parameters%20are%20reoptimized%20at,various%20techniques%2C%20for%20example%2C%20MP2)). This effectively customizes the ansatz per molecule. In summary, UCC ansätze are powerful and give a systematic path to accuracy, but in raw form they’re too resource-intensive for NISQ devices, so iterative or adaptive truncation is recommended to keep circuits short.

- **k-UpCCGSD Ansatz** – **Core structure**: “**Unitary Pair Coupled Cluster with Generalized Singles and Doubles**”, proposed by Lee *et al.* (2018), is a specialized chemistry-inspired ansatz aimed at reducing circuit depth while retaining accuracy ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Unitary%20Pair%20CC%20with%20Generalized,compared%20with%20UCCSD%20because%20it)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Unitary%20pair%20coupled%20cluster%20with,and%20doubles%20product%20wave%20functions)). It is based on a simplified coupled cluster that only uses **pair excitations**: instead of including all distinct single and double excitations, the ansatz includes **paired double excitations** (exciting two electrons from one spatial orbital to another spatial orbital, treating spin-up and spin-down as a pair) plus *generalized singles* (which allow any orbital rotations) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Unitary%20Pair%20CC%20with%20Generalized,to%20unoccupied%20spatial%20orbital%20a)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=correlation%20present%20in%20UCCD%20and,q%20when%20constructing%20excitations%20operators)). Essentially, the cluster operator $T$ is sparsified to include far fewer terms than UCCSD by assuming electrons are excited in correlated pairs. To compensate for the reduced flexibility, the ansatz is applied in a *product of $k$ unitary factors*: i.e., the circuit applies the truncated cluster unitary $k$ times in sequence (with independent parameter sets for each repetition) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ansatz%2C%20a%20product%20of%20k,resulting%20in%20the%20final%20expression)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=number%20of%20missing%20double,resulting%20in%20the%20final%20expression)). For example, a $k=2$ UpCCGSD ansatz means apply the same form of unitary twice, doubling the circuit depth but greatly increasing expressiveness. **Advantages**: The primary advantage is a **dramatic reduction in the number of parameters and gates** needed compared to full UCCSD ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Unitary%20Pair%20CC%20with%20Generalized,compared%20with%20UCCSD%20because%20it)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Unitary%20Pair%20CC%20with%20Generalized,to%20unoccupied%20spatial%20orbital%20a)). By including only the most physically important double excitations (the paired excitations capture strong correlation like bond breaking) and cycling them, the ansatz avoids many terms that contribute little to correlation energy ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=where%20excitations%20are%20performed%20from,occupied%20and%20virtual%20orbitals%20p)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=We%20note%20that%20UCCGSD%20always,resulting%20in%20the%20final%20expression)). The authors showed that for molecules like H₂O, N₂, or a stretched H₄, the k-UpCCGSD ansatz achieved chemical accuracy with far fewer operators than UCCSD or even the fully flexible UCCGSD ansatz (which includes all possible singles and doubles) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,3G)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,but%20requires%20double%20the%20number)). This means shallower circuits and fewer CNOTs, crucial for NISQ viability. Another benefit is that increasing $k$ provides a *systematic improvement* – one can reach arbitrarily high accuracy by increasing the number of repetitions $k$ (in theory, as $k \to \infty$, k-UpCCGSD can approximate the exact wavefunction) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L373%20to%20prepare,improve%20accuracy%20by%20increasing%20k)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=state%20energy%20with%20a%20smaller,3G)). Yet at low $k$, it’s already quite accurate for many systems, making it an efficient ansatz. **Limitations**: The restricted form (paired doubles only) might fail for systems where non-paired excitations (e.g. two electrons excited from different orbitals simultaneously) are important to correlation. Thus k-UpCCGSD is somewhat problem-specific: it works best when correlation is dominated by near-degenerate electron pairs (like in bond breaking scenarios) but might be less effective for chemistry requiring a broader mix of excitations. Also, while still much shorter than UCCSD, the circuit depth grows linearly with $k$ – using, say, $k=4$ might still be challenging on hardware if each repetition is sizeable ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L383%20N_,UpCCGSD)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=state%20energy%20with%20a%20smaller,UpCCGSD)). There’s also an implicit assumption that one repetition of the pair cluster covers the static correlation, and additional repetitions capture dynamic correlation; if that assumption breaks, one might need a larger operator pool. **Suitability for iterative optimization**: k-UpCCGSD introduces an explicit systematic parameter $k$ (number of repetitions) that can be increased iteratively: one could start with $k=1$ (very shallow) and then increase to 2, 3, … until the energy converges. This is simpler than adding entirely new gate types as in ADAPT – one just adds another round of the same ansatz. Practically, one would optimize for each $k$ then use the final parameters as a starting point for $k+1$. Since $k$ essentially adds layers, this is akin to a layer-by-layer growth strategy but with a physics-informed circuit. In summary, k-UpCCGSD is a promising compromise: it leverages physical intuition about paired electrons to keep circuits short, and provides a clear iterative path to improve accuracy by increasing circuit depth gradually ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,3G)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,but%20requires%20double%20the%20number)).

- **ADAPT-VQE (Adaptive Derivative-Assembled Pseudo-Trotter VQE)** – **Core structure**: An **adaptive ansatz** that is not fixed in advance, but built iteratively based on the molecule’s physics during the VQE procedure ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ansatz%20Variational%20Quantum%20Eigensolver%2C%20or,the%20iterative%20algorithm%20for%20the)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L695%20The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)). The algorithm (introduced by Grimsley et al. 2019) begins with a simple initial state (usually the Hartree–Fock state) and an empty or minimal circuit. It then draws from a predefined **operator pool** (commonly a list of possible single or double excitation operators) and at each step selects the operator whose inclusion would most improve the energy (often assessed by the gradient of the energy with respect to adding that operator) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=inspiration%20for%20the%20development%20of,largest%20contribution%20to%20correlation%20energy)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ansatz%20Variational%20Quantum%20Eigensolver%2C%20or,the%20iterative%20algorithm%20for%20the)). The chosen operator (e.g. a specific excitation like $a_i^\dagger a_j$ or a Pauli string equivalent) is added to the ansatz circuit, and all circuit parameters are re-optimized before the next iteration ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ADAPT,smaller%20than%20the%20predefined%20threshold)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)). This process repeats until some convergence criterion is met (e.g. energy improvement below a threshold). Thus, the final ansatz is “assembled” operator-by-operator in a problem-tailored manner. **Advantages**: ADAPT-VQE yields **compact, problem-specific circuits**. Because it only adds the most important excitations, it avoids the redundant rotations with near-zero contribution that plague large UCCSD circuits ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=parameters%20is%20too%20large.%20ADAPT,time%2C%20so%20every%20iteration%20of)). Studies have shown ADAPT-VQE can achieve chemical accuracy with significantly fewer parameters than a comparable static ansatz – for example, in LiH and BeH₂, ADAPT used tens of parameters versus over a hundred in UCCSD while achieving higher accuracy ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20examples%20of%20ADAPT,the%20same%20or%20better%20results)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=internuclear%20distances%2C%20the%20error%20gets,required%20parameters%20grows%20significantly%20for)). Another advantage is **efficiency in optimization**: by building the ansatz gradually, at each step the added parameter can start at a value that locally minimizes energy (often initialized to zero) while previous parameters carry over their optimized values ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L708%20ADAPT,needed%2C%20one%20can%20simply%20add)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ADAPT,needed%2C%20one%20can%20simply%20add)). This means each iteration’s optimization is dealing with a well-behaved landscape (the ansatz is always near a good state) rather than trying to optimize a huge number of parameters from scratch. It’s somewhat analogous to a greedy algorithm that keeps the solution near optimum throughout the build-up. Additionally, the *accuracy is controllable* – one can set a tighter convergence criterion to keep adding operators until the desired precision is reached ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ADAPT,needed%2C%20one%20can%20simply%20add)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ADAPT,needed%2C%20one%20can%20simply%20add)). This adaptability is ideal for leveraging as much circuit as needed but no more. **Limitations**: The flexibility of ADAPT comes at the cost of **computational overhead**. Each iteration requires evaluating the gradient of the energy with respect to *each candidate operator in the pool* to decide which to add ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L719%20count%2C%20which,each%20ADAPT%20iteration%20is%20equal)). If the pool has many operators (which, for $N$ qubits, a full fermionic operator pool can be on the order of $O(N^4)$ and a full qubit Pauli pool is size $2^N-1$ – extremely large ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L779%20compared%20with,the%20strings%20with%20an%20even))), this gradient evaluation step can be expensive in terms of quantum circuit runs (measurements). In practice, heuristics and pruning are used to keep the pool manageable, but the *measurement cost* and overall runtime of ADAPT-VQE can be high, potentially negating the circuit-depth savings by requiring more repetitions of shorter circuits ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=count%2C%20which%20defines%20the%20time,each%20ADAPT%20iteration%20is%20equal)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=number%20of%20CNOT%20gates%20in,P%7D%3Di%5Cprod)). Another limitation is that the final ansatz, while short, might have many parameters that still need global optimization – ADAPT’s advantage is mostly in reducing circuit size, but not necessarily eliminating the possibility of a complex energy landscape for the chosen parameters (though in practice it often helps). There’s also a concern about **resource estimation**: because the ansatz is not fixed upfront, one cannot easily predict how many steps (and thus how many gates or iterations) will be needed, making it harder to anticipate required runtime ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=As%20with%20any%20adaptive%20method%2C,added%20one%20by%20one%2C%20and)). And if one uses a **qubit-ADAPT** variant (Tang et al. 2021) – which uses a pool of elementary Pauli operations to further shorten two-qubit gate depth – the number of parameters tends to increase (since the ansatz breaks down a fermionic excitation into multiple Pauli terms), potentially complicating the optimization and increasing required steps ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=number%20of%20CNOT%20gates%20in,P%7D%3Di%5Cprod)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L772%20with%20fewer,VQE%20approach%20uses)). **Suitability for iterative optimization**: ADAPT-VQE is inherently an *iterative construction paradigm*, so it exemplifies iterative optimization suitability. It adds one circuit element at a time and optimizes continuously, aligning perfectly with an iterative workflow. The ansatz grows in a directed way, ensuring that at each iteration the maximum possible energy improvement is achieved by the new addition ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=inspiration%20for%20the%20development%20of,largest%20contribution%20to%20correlation%20energy)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=ansatz%20Variational%20Quantum%20Eigensolver%2C%20or,the%20iterative%20algorithm%20for%20the)). This greedy approach is quite powerful for NISQ, as it finds efficient use of limited gates. The iterative nature also allows stopping early if the ansatz becomes too large to handle, giving a trade-off between resources and accuracy. In practice, ADAPT can be combined with other iterative techniques, like batching (adding a few operators at once to reduce the number of re-optimization cycles) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L750%20all%20parameters,shot%20count%20is%20to%20pre)) or freezing some parameters during intermediate steps. These hybrid strategies aim to retain ADAPT’s benefits while mitigating costs. Overall, ADAPT-VQE is a highly promising approach for automated ansatz design, particularly as we develop better ways to reduce its overhead (e.g. smarter pool selection, parallel gradient evaluations, or machine learning to predict which operators to add). It directly addresses the question: “Which ansatz do we choose?” by letting the algorithm figure it out dynamically for each molecule.

- **Hamiltonian-Variational and Other Problem-Inspired Ansätze** – **Core structure**: This category encompasses ansatz designs that lie between hardware-efficient and fully chemistry-specific. A prominent example is the **Hamiltonian Variational Ansatz** proposed by Wecker *et al.* (2015) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Shorter%20circuits%20make%20VQE%20more,kind%20lies%20between%20the%20chemistry)), where the structure of the circuit is tied to the terms of the Hamiltonian. In practice, this might mean preparing the state by sequentially applying unitaries of the form $e^{-i \alpha_k P_k}$, where $P_k$ are Pauli terms in the Hamiltonian (or groups of them). Such an ansatz is akin to a **Trotterized adiabatic evolution**: it uses the problem’s own Hamiltonian (or components of it) as the building blocks of the trial state ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Shorter%20circuits%20make%20VQE%20more,kind%20lies%20between%20the%20chemistry)). Another related approach is the **Quantum Approximate Optimization Ansatz (QAOA)** – while typically used for combinatorial optimization, in form it alternates applying the problem Hamiltonian and a mixing Hamiltonian for a certain number of layers. For a chemistry Hamiltonian, one could conceive a QAOA-like ansatz where you alternate between evolving under the molecular Hamiltonian and some mixer that maintains particle number. These Hamiltonian-inspired ansätze thus incorporate knowledge of the problem’s operator structure. **Advantages**: They naturally **embed problem structure**, which can make them more efficient than random circuits. By using Hamiltonian terms, one ensures the ansatz can reach states that are connected through the Hamiltonian’s dynamics (which should include the ground state). They can also be more hardware-efficient than a full UCC, because each term’s unitary might be implementable with fewer gates than an arbitrary excitation – especially if the Hamiltonian has a lot of local structure or if one can truncate to dominant terms. Additionally, if one uses a shallow depth (few trotter steps or QAOA layers), the circuit is short and the number of parameters is small (each layer may have just a handful of angles corresponding to different terms). **Limitations**: The expressiveness of Hamiltonian-based ansätze can be limited by the order of terms and depth. For example, a single Trotter-step ansatz of $e^{-i\vec{\alpha}\cdot \vec{H}}$ (a linear combination of Hamiltonian exponentials) might not capture the ground state well unless the initial state has large overlap with it. Essentially, these ansätze need enough layers (repetitions) to approximate an arbitrary state, which can start to resemble the problem of adiabatic state preparation requiring long evolutions. Another issue is that applying Hamiltonian exponentials exactly may still need many gates if the Hamiltonian has long Pauli strings (though techniques exist to compile them). And unlike UCC, the parameters here don’t correspond directly to “amplitudes” of configurations, so interpreting or guiding the optimization can be harder. **Suitability for iterative optimization**: Much like QAOA, one can treat the number of layers as an iterative parameter – start with 1 layer (which might not be very accurate, but is easy to optimize), then increase to 2 layers, and so on. Each added layer introduces a few more parameters and can variationally improve the energy. This is a natural iterative scheme: layer $p+1$ QAOA typically improves on layer $p$. The risk, however, is that optimization may get harder with more layers (as parameter landscapes become more intricate). But some studies use **layer-by-layer training** (optimize one layer at a time, freeze it, then add another) to mitigate this. Overall, Hamiltonian or problem-inspired ansätze provide a middle ground: they respect some problem topology, often yielding better initial guesses and faster convergence than hardware-agnostic circuits, while still keeping circuits relatively shallow. They can be a good starting point which, if insufficient, can be hybridized or augmented by more flexible layers.

*(In addition to the above, there are numerous other ansätze and hybrid strategies in the literature – e.g., **Low-Depth Circuit Ansätze (LDCA)** designed for specific correlation regimes ([www.researchgate.net](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363850800_The_Variational_Quantum_Eigensolver_A_review_of_methods_and_best_practices#:~:text=polaritonic%20chemistry%20,be%20implemented%20as%20an%20ansatz)), **Orbital-Optimized ansätze** that allow the single-particle basis to adjust along with cluster parameters ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Orbital%20optimized%20UCC%20%28OO)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=%28here%20%5C%28%5Chat%20,that%20only%20doubles%20are%20included)), and approaches incorporating **classical preprocessing** (like freezing core electrons or using multireference trial states). There are also entirely different strategies such as quantum machine learning models repurposed for chemistry, or pulse-level ansätze (where one optimizes analog control pulses directly rather than discrete gate sequences ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.04913#:~:text=PAN%20It%20was%20the%20first,PAN%20will%20first%20extract)) ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.04913#:~:text=building%20ansatz%2C%20you%20need%20to,the%20optimizer%20to%20ensure%20the))). Each has its own pros and cons, but for brevity, we focus on the most prominent categories above.)*

## Exploratory Investigation

### Historical Perspective on Ansatz Design in Quantum Chemistry

To fully appreciate modern VQE ansätze, it’s useful to trace the origins of **wavefunction ansatz design** in quantum chemistry. Long before quantum computers, chemists faced the same fundamental problem: how to efficiently approximate the ground state of a molecular Hamiltonian, given that exact solutions (full configuration interaction) scale exponentially with system size. The *variational principle* – dating back to Lord Rayleigh and Weierstrass in the 19th century and applied to quantum systems by the early 20th century – became a cornerstone approach. Under the variational principle, one posits a family of trial wavefunctions $|\psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle$ with adjustable parameters and then minimizes the expectation $\langle \psi(\vec{\theta})|H|\psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle$ to find the best approximation of the ground state within that family. This is conceptually the same strategy VQE uses ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=successfully%20applied%20to%20solve%20the,recent%20developments%20in%20the%20field)), albeit with a quantum circuit preparing $|\psi(\vec{\theta})\rangle$ instead of an analytic function.

**Early ansätze** in quantum chemistry were often inspired by physical intuition. For example, in 1927, Heitler and London wrote the first wavefunction for the $H_2$ molecule as a combination of atomic orbitals – essentially an ansatz capturing the idea that electrons pair between two hydrogen atoms. This was followed by Linus Pauling’s and others’ **Valence Bond** ansätze and the **Molecular Orbital** ansatz (1930s), where one electron occupies a delocalized orbital over the molecule. Each of these was a simple guess of a wavefunction form. The **Hartree–Fock (HF) method** (1930s) formalized ansatz design by using a single *Slater determinant* (an antisymmetrized product of one-electron orbitals) as the trial wavefunction ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=Two%20highly%20productive%20approaches%20to,the%20localized%20version%20of%20this)) ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=Hartree%E2%80%93Fock%20theory%20is%20the%20simplest,order%20perturbation)). The parameters to optimize were the shapes of these orbitals (usually expanded in a basis), found by solving the self-consistent field equations. HF provided a mean-field ansatz – capturing the average effect of electron interactions – but it neglects instantaneous electron correlation, often leading to significant errors in bond energies and reaction barriers ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=and%20hence%2C%20a%20brute%20force,these%20lines%20is%20outlined%20below)).

To improve upon Hartree–Fock, chemists introduced **post-Hartree–Fock ansätze** which explicitly include *electron correlation*. A major category is Configuration Interaction (CI), where the ansatz is a *linear combination* of Slater determinants. For instance, CI singles and doubles (CISD) uses a superposition of the HF determinant plus all singly and doubly excited determinants (with respect to HF). The coefficients of this linear combination are variational parameters. CI is systematically improvable (include more determinants up to full CI which is exact), but the number of determinants grows combinatorially with system size, making CI impractical beyond small molecules. However, CI conceptually influenced quantum algorithms: a quantum register naturally can exist in a superposition of basis states akin to a CI expansion. If one could prepare a state that is a superposition of many determinants with the correct amplitudes, that would be essentially solving CI. In fact, the full CI ground state is what VQE ultimately tries to approximate using a clever ansatz that doesn’t enumerate all determinants explicitly.

Another landmark ansatz was the **Coupled Cluster (CC) ansatz**, introduced by Čížek and others in the 1960s ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=perturbation%20theory%20,for%20different%20types%20of%20problems)) ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=Coupled%20cluster%20methods%20are%20the,Thus)). Instead of a linear combination, CC uses an *exponential* ansatz: $|\Psi_{CC}\rangle = e^T | \Phi_{\rm HF}\rangle$, where $T$ is the cluster operator (a sum of excitation operators similar to those in CI). Expanding $e^T$ generates an infinite series of determinants (including high excitations) but in a compact, non-linear way that often converges faster and more accurately than truncated CI. For example, a CC single and double (CCSD) ansatz includes connected single and double excitations in $T$, but $e^T$ also effectively includes certain triple, quadruple, etc. excitations via the exponential expansion. This **exponential ansatz** was hugely successful – CCSD and its perturbative triples variant CCSD(T) became the “gold standard” for many quantum chemistry problems ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=Coupled%20cluster%20methods%20are%20the,Thus)) ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=In%20return%20for%20this%20high,13)), achieving high accuracy for small to medium molecules, albeit at high computational cost (CCSD scales ~N^6, CCSD(T) ~N^7 in computational steps). The key takeaway is that an exponential ansatz can incorporate correlation *non-linearly* and more efficiently than a linear ansatz (CI) ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=Coupled%20cluster%20methods%20are%20the,Thus)). This insight directly carries over to quantum computing: the Unitary Coupled Cluster (UCC) ansatz is basically the unitary version of the CC ansatz ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20first%20VQE%20experiment%20by,hat%20%7BT%7D%5E%7B%5Cdagger)). Classical CC is not variational when truncated (because $\langle \Psi_{CC}|H|\Psi_{CC}\rangle$ can’t be evaluated easily for an exponential times a non-variational left state), but UCC is variational (since $\langle\Psi|$ is just the unitary applied to a reference, one can variationally minimize the energy). The catch is, on a classical computer, UCC with many parameters is even harder to solve than CC because of the non-terminating Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion needed to compute $\langle \Psi_{UCC}|H|\Psi_{UCC}\rangle$ ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1420-3049/30/1/119#:~:text=Evaluating%20Variational%20Quantum%20Eigensolver%20Approaches,makes%20it%20possible%20to%20map)). This is exactly where quantum computers come in: they can in principle *prepare* the UCC state and measure the energy without having to expand the exponential on a classical machine. Thus, one of the earliest proposals (and the first VQE experiment by Peruzzo et al. in 2014) used the UCCSD ansatz as a starting point ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20first%20VQE%20experiment%20by,hat%20%7BT%7D%5E%7B%5Cdagger)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=%24%24%20%5Chat,dagg%20er%7D_%7Bj%7D%5Chat%7Ba%7D_%7B%5Cbeta%7D%20%5Chat%7Ba%7D_%7B%5Calpha%7D.)), marrying a classical chemistry ansatz with quantum optimization.

Beyond CC, other classical ansätze include **Multi-Reference methods** (like CASSCF – Complete Active Space Self-Consistent Field – which selects a subset of orbitals to treat with a full CI ansatz while the rest remain at HF level). This is akin to having a tailored ansatz that focuses on strongly correlated electrons with a full flexible form, and a simple ansatz for others. Ideas like that inform quantum strategies too – for example, one might use a small active space on a quantum computer and treat the rest classically (quantum-classical embedding), thereby simplifying the ansatz for the quantum part. Additionally, **Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC)** methods introduced yet another ansatz form: the inclusion of **Jastrow factors**, which are explicit correlation functions (often exponentials of inter-electron distances) multiplying a Slater determinant. Jastrow factors account for things like the cusp condition (electron-electron repulsion effects) in a very compact way. While implementing a continuous Jastrow factor on a quantum circuit is not straightforward, some research has explored whether certain Jastrow-like corrections or symmetry projections can be compiled into an ansatz to capture dynamic correlation with fewer gates.

In summary, by the time quantum computing for chemistry came along, the community already had a rich toolkit of ansätze: from mean-field single determinants, through linear expansions of determinants, to exponential cluster operators, and even hybrid forms. A strong theme was **balancing accuracy and computational cost** – an echo of the same trade-off we face in VQE between ansatz expressiveness and hardware feasibility ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1420-3049/30/1/119#:~:text=quantum%20systems%20,without%20incurring%20excessive%20computational%20costs)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1420-3049/30/1/119#:~:text=performance%20and%20resource%20utilization,variational%20algorithms%20in%20quantum%20computing)). Each classical ansatz was essentially a different point in this balance: HF was cheap but low accuracy; full CI was exact but intractable; CCSD(T) hit a sweet spot for many cases (high accuracy at high but manageable cost for small systems) ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=Coupled%20cluster%20methods%20are%20the,Thus)) ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=In%20return%20for%20this%20high,13)).

### Emergence of VQE and Early Ansatz Choices

When quantum algorithms for chemistry were first contemplated, the initial star was not VQE but **Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE)**. The QPE algorithm (developed in the 1990s) can find eigenvalues of a unitary operator – for chemistry, one makes the Hamiltonian evolution $e^{-iHt}$ into a unitary and uses QPE to get eigen-energies ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20first%20algorithm%20that%20was,It%20is)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=unitary%20operators%2C%20such%20as%20the,capabilities%20of%20present%20NISQ%20hardware)). QPE essentially performs a high-level **projective measurement** of energy given a proper eigenstate as input. The big challenge for QPE is you need to start in a state with a substantial overlap with the true ground state; otherwise the algorithm will collapse onto whichever eigenstate was actually prepared. Preparing such a state was non-trivial – essentially, one still needed an ansatz or a method to get close to the ground state. In practice, one approach was **adiabatic state preparation**: start from an easy-to-prepare state (like Hartree–Fock, which is just a computational basis state after Jordan–Wigner mapping) and then slowly evolve under a time-dependent Hamiltonian that morphs into the target Hamiltonian. If done slowly enough, the adiabatic theorem guarantees you’ll end in the ground state of the target Hamiltonian. Adiabatic preparation is akin to an ansatz path – it doesn’t have variational parameters, rather a time schedule, but conceptually it’s another strategy to reach the ground state. However, adiabatic algorithms and QPE both demanded long coherent evolutions, far out of reach of early quantum hardware (and even today, full QPE requires error-corrected quantum computers with millions of logical qubits for chemistry applications ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=needed%20to%20solve%20any%20useful,case%20for%20the%20near%20future)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=unitary%20operators%2C%20such%20as%20the,capabilities%20of%20present%20NISQ%20hardware))).

This context set the stage for VQE: in 2014 Peruzzo et al. proposed offloading the state preparation problem to a hybrid optimization, where you *assume* a certain circuit form (ansatz) and then let a classical optimizer find the best parameters ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=To%20address%20this%20problem%2C%20a,23%20Harsha%20et%20al)). The insight was that short circuits can prepare a decent approximation, and any remaining error can be corrected by adjusting the parameters iteratively, which is more feasible on NISQ machines than long fixed evolutions. Crucially, VQE inverts the QPE requirement: instead of needing the state preparation to be almost perfect, you can start with a very imperfect trial state and correct it step by step using measurement feedback. The only requirement is that your ansatz family is flexible enough to eventually include a state with high overlap with the true ground state (to satisfy the variational principle’s potential to reach the ground truth). Thus, ansatz design became the *central question*: what circuits can represent the ground states of interesting molecules while being shallow enough to run on hardware?

The first implementations of VQE (Peruzzo’s experiment on a small photonic quantum processor in 2014, and later O’Malley et al. on superconducting qubits in 2016) chose the **Unitary Coupled Cluster (UCC) ansatz**, specifically UCCSD, as the trial state form ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20first%20VQE%20experiment%20by,hat%20%7BT%7D%5E%7B%5Cdagger)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=%24%24%20%5Chat,dagg%20er%7D_%7Bj%7D%5Chat%7Ba%7D_%7B%5Cbeta%7D%20%5Chat%7Ba%7D_%7B%5Calpha%7D.)). There were clear reasons for this choice: UCCSD was well-known in classical chemistry to give good results for molecules like H₂, LiH, etc., at least around equilibrium geometries. It also uses far fewer parameters than a generic state on the same number of qubits (for instance, LiH with 4 qubits in the active space may have only ~12-15 parameters in UCCSD, versus millions of parameters to describe an arbitrary 4-qubit state vector). This parameter efficiency was thought to aid the classical optimization. Moreover, being chemistry-inspired, UCCSD ensured that if the Hartree–Fock state is already a decent starting point, the remaining correlation can be added perturbatively via small parameters, which is a gentle landscape for classical optimization (initially, all cluster amplitudes can start at 0) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=parameters%20is%20too%20large.%20ADAPT,time%2C%20so%20every%20iteration%20of)). Indeed, those early VQE experiments succeeded in computing the energy of H₂, LiH and even BeH₂ within chemical accuracy, validating both the VQE approach and the UCC ansatz on actual hardware (albeit with only 2–4 qubits) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=hardware%20architectures%20%28Peruzzo%20et%20al,variational%20parameters%2C%20which%20can%20render)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=translates%20into%20massive%20quantum%20circuits%2C,standard%20UCCSD%20and%20hardware%E2%80%93efficient%20ansatzes)).

However, as researchers tried to scale up to bigger molecules or even the same small molecules but with realistic basis sets, the issues with UCCSD ansätze became apparent. The circuits blew up in depth – e.g., one study estimated that a straightforward UCCSD for even a modest molecule like $\text{Fe}_2\text{S}_2$ would require circuits with thousands of gates, which is utterly infeasible on current devices. By 2017, an important experimental paper by Kandala et al. (IBM) took a different route: they introduced the term **“hardware-efficient ansatz”** and demonstrated VQEs for small molecules using a hardware-efficient trial state instead of a chemistry-inspired one ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=Reference)). Their ansatz was a series of $R_y$ rotations on each qubit followed by a ladder of CNOT entanglers (essentially a shallow unitary that creates an entangled state with a handful of parameters). The aim was to adapt the ansatz to the hardware’s strengths (short gate sequences, connectivity-compatible entangling gates). They successfully found ground-state energies for H₂, LiH, and even some simple model of BeH₂ using this approach ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=%2A%20%22Hardware,Nature%20549%2C%20242%E2%80%93246%20%282017)). The results were promising, but also revealed new challenges: unlike UCC, the hardware-efficient ansatz had no built-in notion of particle number or spin, so they had to include extra ancilla qubits and penalties in the cost function to enforce the correct number of electrons. Additionally, the optimization faced more difficulty – the term “barren plateau” was coined around this time (2018) by McClean et al., who showed that for certain random circuits the gradients vanish exponentially with qubit count and depth ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=hardware,efficient)). Hardware-efficient ansätze with enough random entanglement can fall into this trap. In practice, careful initialization and problem-informed tweaks (like starting in a state with the right number of electrons) were needed to get the algorithm to converge on the correct energy.

The contrast between UCCSD and hardware-efficient approaches highlighted a **dilemma**: do we choose an ansatz that’s *efficient to optimize (chemistry-informed)* or one that’s *efficient to implement (hardware-informed)*? This motivated many of the developments in the late 2010s and early 2020s, as researchers sought ways to get the best of both worlds.

### Advances in Ansatz Design: Hybrid and Adaptive Approaches

One line of attack was to refine the *chemistry-inspired ansatz* to make it more hardware-friendly. For example:

- The **k-UpCCGSD ansatz** (2018) we discussed in the comparison is one such refinement: by drastically reducing the number of excitation terms (only pair excitations) but compensating with multiple repetitions, it cuts down circuit depth dramatically ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Unitary%20Pair%20CC%20with%20Generalized,to%20unoccupied%20spatial%20orbital%20a)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,but%20requires%20double%20the%20number)). Testing showed it reached better accuracy than UCCSD with fewer gates for several small molecules ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=The%20k,3G)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=state%20energy%20with%20a%20smaller,UpCCGSD)). Essentially, it was a clever way to remove the insignificant pieces of UCCSD and concentrate on the important ones, leveraging insights from strong correlation (like in bond dissociation where electrons pair off).

- Another idea was **Orbital Optimization** in the ansatz (OO-UCC, 2020) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Orbital%20optimized%20UCC%20%28OO)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=e,array%7D)). In classical post-HF methods, allowing the orbital basis to rotate (like the difference between HF and Brueckner orbitals, or doing a state-specific orbital optimization in multi-reference methods) can yield a more compact wavefunction. OO-UCC essentially adds additional variational parameters to rotate the single-particle basis (the molecular orbitals) along with the cluster operator ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Orbital%20optimized%20UCC%20%28OO)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=)). This can absorb some correlation effects into the basis, thereby simplifying the cluster part. The reported results showed modest reductions in circuit depth and parameter count for similar accuracy ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=rotations%20that%20would%20be%20variationally,3)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=molecule%20with%20the%20STO,results%20than%20to%20UCCSD)). In effect, OO-UCC shifts some of the burden to classical preprocessing (finding a better orbital set) so the quantum circuit doesn’t have to work as hard.

- Yet another is the **Downfolded or Effective Hamiltonian approaches (DUCC)**, where one integrates out high-energy excitations and produces an effective Hamiltonian for a smaller subspace, then uses a corresponding ansatz ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Orbital%20optimized%20UCC%20%28OO)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L689%20%24%24%20%7C%7B%5Cpsi,1%7D%7D%5Cright%29...%5Cleft%28e%5E%7B%5Chat%7B%5Ctau%7D_%7B2%7D%7D%5Cright%29%20%5Cleft%28e%5E%7B%5Chat%7B%5Ctau%7D_%7B1%7D%7D%5Cright%29%20%7C%7B%5Cphi%7D%5Crangle.)). The *Driven Unitary Coupled Cluster (DUCC)* method by Kowalski’s group, for instance, identifies a subspace (active space) and treats its complement via unitary transformations to derive a smaller Hamiltonian whose ground state is the same as the full one within certain approximation. The ansatz for the reduced problem can then be simpler. This moves complexity from the circuit to classical computations of the effective Hamiltonian. It’s a form of **quantum/classical hybridization,** continuing the long-standing quantum chemistry practice of reducing problem size via embedding or active spaces.

On the other side, researchers improved **hardware-efficient ansätze** by incorporating more problem structure or mitigating their issues:

- One improvement was to explicitly encode **symmetries**. For example, one can modify a hardware-efficient ansatz to only produce states with the correct number of 1s (electrons) in the binary representation (qubit state). This can be done by choosing entangling patterns that swap excitation between qubits while conserving total excitation count, or by using techniques like **hard-core boson mappings**. An example is a “problem-tailored hardware-efficient ansatz” where, say, half the qubits start in $|1\rangle$ (occupied) and half in $|0\rangle$ (virtual), and the entanglers only exchange excitations between those sets rather than create/destroy excitations arbitrarily ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1420-3049/30/1/119#:~:text=Ansatzes%201%20and%204%20can,by%20a%20similar%20number%20of)) ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1420-3049/30/1/119#:~:text=,freedom%20than%20the%20previous%20one)). The snippet from the MDPI study ([www.mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/1420-3049/30/1/119#:~:text=Ansatzes%201%20and%204%20can,all%20the%20orbitals%2C%20but%20when)) indeed describes ansätze where the first set of qubits represent occupied orbitals and second represent virtual orbitals, and the circuit entangles them in a way that mimics excitations (occupied-virtual mixing and spin mixing). This kind of design brings a chemical interpretation to a hardware layer structure, ensuring meaningful states are explored. The result is a ansatz that’s still relatively shallow but more **expressive for chemistry** than a completely uninformed one.

- **Reducing parameter count** in hardware-efficient ansätze without losing too much expressibility has also been studied. Too many parameters can slow down classical optimization. Some ansatz variants tie parameters together (for example, using the same rotation angle on multiple qubits for symmetry reasons) or eliminate redundant rotations. This is partly inspired by classical neural networks, where one might reduce parameters via symmetries or sparse connections to avoid overfitting – analogously in quantum circuits, fewer parameters can mean an easier training landscape.

- A significant challenge – barren plateaus – has multiple mitigation techniques that intertwine with ansatz design. One approach is **layer-wise training** (as mentioned): rather than random initialization for all layers, one can optimize a single layer ansatz, then add a second layer initialized to identity, optimize both, and so on. This was shown to avoid the plateau phenomenon to some extent, because each expansion starts from a good point. Another approach is using **local cost functions** or **local measurements** during training in place of a fully global energy cost, to provide higher-gradient signals (this ventures beyond pure ansatz design into algorithm design, but it influences how we structure the ansatz too, perhaps grouping parameters to target specific terms).

Probably the most groundbreaking developments were the **Adaptive ansätze** like ADAPT-VQE and its variants (introduced around 2019-2020) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=Grimsley%20et%20al,Trotter)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L695%20The%20ADAPT,a%20result%2C%20the%20quantum%20circuits)). We’ve covered ADAPT-VQE in detail, but it’s worth noting how it opened a new paradigm: **algorithmically constructing the ansatz** as opposed to guessing it ahead of time. ADAPT-VQE’s success in producing minimal ansätze sparked others to refine the idea:

- **Qubit-ADAPT-VQE** (Tang et al. 2021), which we mentioned, replaced the pool of fermionic excitation operators with a pool of qubit Pauli operators in order to generate circuits with fewer CNOT gates ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=number%20of%20CNOT%20gates%20in,P%7D%3Di%5Cprod)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L772%20with%20fewer,VQE%20approach%20uses)). Each selected Pauli exponentially is simpler (more directly translates to a small gate sequence) than a fermionic excitation which might require many CNOTs to implement via Jordan-Wigner strings. The trade-off was needing more terms (since a single fermionic excitation maps to a sum of Pauli strings). Qubit-ADAPT successfully cut gate counts, but at the expense of more optimization parameters and more measurements to find them. It essentially **shifted complexity** from quantum runtime to classical runtime – a reasonable trade in the NISQ era, provided the classical side (gradient computations, etc.) remains tractable ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=with%20fewer%20CNOT%20gates%20compared,VQE%20approach%20uses)) ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L796%20measurement%20overhead,6%7D%2C%20and%20LiH%20molecules)).

- **Adaptive “batching” or gradient-informed pruning**: Realizing the bottleneck of ADAPT is measuring gradients for all operators each iteration, some works propose smarter ways to choose the next operator. For example, instead of scanning the entire pool every time, one could use information from previous iterations to predict which few operators are most likely to have large gradients (perhaps via machine learning or heuristic rules). There’s also research on adding multiple operators per iteration (if they commute or approximately commute) to reduce the number of optimization cycles needed – effectively a mini-batch of ansatz growth to speed up convergence ([jmsh.springeropen.com](https://jmsh.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41313-021-00032-6#:~:text=match%20at%20L750%20all%20parameters,shot%20count%20is%20to%20pre)).

- **Overlap-ADAPT-VQE** (2023, mentioned in search results) and others: these are modifications of ADAPT that use different criteria than energy gradient to choose new operators. Overlap-ADAPT, for instance, chooses operators that maximize overlap with some target wavefunction piece, claiming to build even more compact ansätze.

Parallel to ADAPT, there were attempts to use **machine learning (ML) to design ansätze**. Two notable directions:

1. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) for circuit construction**: In an RL framework, one treats the selection of gates for an ansatz as a sequential decision process. An agent (maybe a deep neural network) is trained to add gates one by one to minimize the final energy ([openreview.net](https://openreview.net/forum?id=JQznhE5mdyv#:~:text=architectures%20,Quantum%20Computing%2C%20Reinforcement%20Learning%2C%20Quantum)) ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.16089#:~:text=,This%20assumption%20in%20practice%20is)). The state can encode what gates are already in the ansatz and how well it’s doing, and actions are choices of next gate or parameter tweaks. The reward is higher when the energy is lower (or negative of energy as a reward). Ostaszewski *et al.* (2021) used such an approach to optimize variational circuit architectures, demonstrating that an RL agent could autonomously discover circuit structures that rival hand-designed ansätze for certain small problems ([openreview.net](https://openreview.net/forum?id=JQznhE5mdyv#:~:text=architectures%20,Quantum%20Computing%2C%20Reinforcement%20Learning%2C%20Quantum)). RL can explore non-intuitive ansatz structures, potentially discovering novel gate sequences that human designers might not guess. However, training an RL agent requires many simulations of the quantum circuit (or at least a lot of data from experiments), so it’s currently limited to smaller systems in simulation. The hope is as classical simulators and smaller quantum devices provide data, an AI could learn patterns and then generalize to propose ansätze for larger cases that we haven’t solved yet.

2. **Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs)**: Some works use genetic algorithms or other evolutionary strategies to evolve a population of circuit designs toward lower energy. Circuits (ansätze) can be encoded as sequences of genes (each gene could be a gate or a layer configuration), and one defines mutation operations (randomly add/remove/change a gate) and crossover operations (combine parts of two circuits). The “fitness” is how low the energy gets with optimal parameters (obtained by a subroutine running a short VQE for that circuit). Over many generations, the population hopefully converges to a highly optimized ansatz. This approach has been tried in a few studies; it’s very computationally intensive but conceptually appealing because it mimics how one might perform trial-and-error refinement of circuits automatically.

Another worth mentioning is **“Quantum Circuit Learning”** (the method by Ostaszewski, Benedetti et al., 2019) ([quantaggle.com](https://quantaggle.com/algorithms/ansatz/#:~:text=Quantum%20circuit%20structure%20learning)), which essentially did a gradient-based structure optimization: it allowed for removing or inserting single-qubit rotations in a circuit by treating their presence/absence as differentiable parameters (some clever trick of assigning continuous parameters that smoothly go to zero for removal). It’s technical, but the outcome is the algorithm can *learn both the parameters and the structure* of a quantum circuit ansatz. This was demonstrated on some simple problems and shares the same goal as ADAPT and RL – letting the computer find the ansatz structure.

### Insights from Quantum Chemistry and New Directions

Given all these developments, what *new directions or ideas* can we draw from outside the immediate VQE literature? The user’s request emphasizes looking “as far back in history as possible” and across related fields, so here are a few broader considerations:

- **Inspiration from Classical Methods**: We touched on many classical ansätze (HF, CI, CC, etc.). One classical approach not yet fully leveraged in VQE is **Density Functional Theory (DFT)**, which is an alternate paradigm where the key object is the electron density rather than the wavefunction ([www.pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.0408036102#:~:text=The%20second%20class%20of%20theoretical,Initial)). DFT doesn’t construct a wavefunction ansatz at all; it finds the energy through a functional of the density. While a quantum computer naturally works with wavefunctions, there’s ongoing research into whether some DFT-like strategies (using qubits to represent densities or using quantum resources to find better functionals) could circumvent the need for large wavefunction ansätze. It’s an open question if a hybrid DFT-VQE approach could be formulated (for example, using a coarse density functional to guide a few parameters of a wavefunction ansatz).

- **Tensor Network Ansätze**: In classical simulations of quantum many-body physics (especially in condensed matter), *tensor network states* such as Matrix Product States (MPS) and the Multi-scale Entanglement Renormalization Ansatz (MERA) have proven extremely powerful. These ansätze are designed to efficiently capture the entanglement structure of certain systems (e.g., one-dimensional chains for MPS, or systems with a notion of scale for MERA). They are essentially variational ansätze with certain entanglement patterns and polynomial parameters in system size (avoiding exponential blow-up by restricting structure). In fact, an MPS with low bond dimension is an example of an ansatz that might be representable by a shallow quantum circuit (because an MPS can be prepared sequentially with a circuit of depth proportional to number of qubits if bond dimension is small). Some researchers have tried to use quantum circuits to represent MPS or PEPS (Projected Entangled Pair States for 2D) as ansätze for VQE, especially for lattice models or translationally invariant systems. For molecules, one might imagine using a tensor network ansatz to capture locality in real-space or some fragmentation of the molecule. This hasn’t been mainstream in VQE for chemistry yet, because molecules don’t naturally map onto 1D spin chains (one can always impose an ordering though). But as NISQ devices grow, exploring ansätze that borrow from tensor networks (which essentially build in a lot of entanglement structure but limit long-range entanglement) could be fruitful. These might be seen as advanced hardware-efficient ansätze that intentionally restrict entanglement to certain patterns to remain trainable.

- **Explicit Correlation and Continuum Ansätze**: Traditional quantum chemistry has methods to handle what’s called “highly-correlated electrons” or continuum degrees (like in Rydberg states or when electrons approach each other). *Explicitly correlated (F12) methods* include terms like $e^{-\alpha r_{ij}}$ in the ansatz to account for cusp conditions. In a quantum circuit context, incorporating such continuous correlation functions might be approached by using ancilla qubits or qubit readouts to apply corrections for close electron distances. While not straightforward, if someone could design an ansatz that inherently obeys the $r_{ij}\to 0$ cusp condition (thus capturing the divergence in kinetic energy correctly), it could vastly improve basis set convergence, meaning one could use smaller basis sets (fewer qubits) for the same accuracy. Some work in this direction involves *transcorrelated Hamiltonians*: a similarity transformation of the Hamiltonian by a Jastrow operator, which means the ansatz is effectively a Jastrow+Slater form but the Hamiltonian gains extra terms. Solving that on a quantum computer could be an alternative to solving the original Hamiltonian with a simpler ansatz.

- **Analog vs Digital Ansätze**: All we discussed assumes a digital gate model quantum computer. However, analog quantum simulators (like cold atom systems, quantum annealers, etc.) naturally evolve under certain Hamiltonians. A future direction might be to combine analog techniques with digital VQE: for example, use an analog simulator to prepare a state that is a good ansatz for a particular type of Hamiltonian (like using a trapped-ion system in analog mode to realize an approximate ground state via cooling or adiabatic prep, then input that state into a digital circuit for fine variational tweaking). This is speculative, but as part of exploring new directions, one can consider *hybrid analog-digital ansätze*. Already, at the pulse level, some researchers talk about **pulse-efficient or analog ansatz**: directly optimizing control pulses to prepare the state, rather than a predetermined gate sequence ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.04913#:~:text=PAN%20It%20was%20the%20first,PAN%20will%20first%20extract)) ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.04913#:~:text=building%20ansatz%2C%20you%20need%20to,the%20optimizer%20to%20ensure%20the)). This can bypass layers of gate compilation and perhaps exploit device physics better (e.g., turning on a two-qubit analog interaction for an optimal duration). The mentioned **Pulse Ansatz (PAN)** by Shi and Qian (2022) is an example where they build ansätze in the analog control domain ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.04913#:~:text=PAN%20It%20was%20the%20first,PAN%20will%20first%20extract)) ([ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.04913#:~:text=building%20ansatz%2C%20you%20need%20to,the%20optimizer%20to%20ensure%20the)), achieving the same performance with significantly shorter pulse durations compared to standard gate ansätze. While this strays from the typical gate-focused discussion, it’s a direction that could yield more efficient state preparation if the control problem can be solved variationally.

- **Scaling toward fault tolerance**: While NISQ techniques dominate now, it’s worth considering what ansatz design might look like once we have error-corrected quantum computers. If errors are not a limiting factor, depth becomes less critical and one might revisit more powerful ansätze that are currently impractical. For example, one could implement deeper circuits for UCC including triple and quadruple excitations systematically, or perform QPE with a short-depth variational circuit to generate the initial state. Even in that era, ansatz efficiency matters because the number of logical qubits and gates might still be a bottleneck. It might turn out that some of the techniques developed now (like adaptive construction or ML-designed circuits) will be employed to reduce the cost even on fault-tolerant devices, simply to cut down on runtime and required qubits (since error correction overhead is huge). In other words, the philosophy of “problem-tailored ansatz” is likely to remain valuable. We might see libraries of ansatz circuits for different molecule classes, much like we have basis set libraries in classical chemistry.

- **AI-driven automated ansatz design**: Finally, the overarching theme of the user’s query is to gather knowledge for AI-driven ansatz design. Based on the survey above, an AI system could take into account multiple aspects: it might analyze the molecular Hamiltonian (graph structure of interacting orbitals, magnitudes of integrals) and decide whether a hardware-efficient or chemistry-inspired or hybrid ansatz is likely to perform better. It could predict which subset of excitation operators are most important (maybe using classical electron correlation theories as a guide). It could use reinforcement learning to assemble gate sequences that implement those excitations with minimal overhead. It might also consider hardware specifics (connectivity, gate fidelities) to tailor the ansatz to the *particular quantum device* that will run it, which is an often practical but sometimes overlooked aspect in academic proposals. Ultimately, an AI-driven approach might output a custom ansatz for each new molecule or material, perhaps even constructing it in a interpretable way (e.g., “use 5 layers of this entangler pattern, add these 3 specific excitation gates, etc.”). This would automate what is currently done by human researchers tuning and testing different ansätze.

The historical progress and current research show that **ansatz design is a synergy of physics, chemistry, and computer science**. It requires understanding the physical system (to capture its state efficiently), knowledge of quantum hardware (to respect practical limits), and algorithmic innovation (to navigate the enormous design space). By surveying developments from the early days of quantum theory to the latest adaptive algorithms, we see a unifying goal: find representations of the ground state that are as compact as possible. Variational quantum algorithms are the latest vehicle for this old quest, and ansatz design is where much of the creativity and interdisciplinary exchange happens. Moving forward, leveraging AI to explore this design space is a natural progression – the complexity of the problem (exponential possibilities) suggests that heuristic or learned strategies will be invaluable for proposing ansatz structures that human intuition might miss. With comprehensive reference to known ansatz types (from hardware-efficient to UCC and adaptive methods) and lessons from decades of quantum chemistry, an AI-driven ansatz designer can be guided to avoid known pitfalls (like barren plateaus or symmetry violations) and incorporate known best practices (like starting from physically meaningful reference states or using compact operator sets). The continued evolution of ansätze will likely involve hybridizing ideas: for instance, combining a hardware-efficient scaffold with a few problem-specific gates (sometimes called a “problem-aware hardware ansatz”), or using coarse AI suggestions refined by an adaptive loop.
